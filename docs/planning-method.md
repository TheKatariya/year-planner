# The Year Planning Method

The approach behind the tool. Adapted from Blended Athletics' "Plan Your Whole Damn Year"
(kimberley-hue.github.io/big-ass-calendar) — the planner is a rebuild, this is the thinking it was
built to support.

Read this once before your first planning session. Re-read the **Seven Steps** and **The 90-Minute
Meeting** each time you sit down to plan a year.

---

## The problem it solves

The failure mode isn't a shortage of ideas. It's seeing the year too late.

> "We should run something this summer."
> → Suddenly it's June.
> → Someone needs to design it.
> → Marketing needs copy.
> → Staff needs information.
> → Members need notice.
> → Registration needs to open.
> → And now we're already behind.

Most owners look at today, this week, this month. Planning the major pieces ahead of time gives
marketing, staff, operations and members the time they actually need to prepare.

**What changes when you can see the whole year at once:**

- See slow periods before they arrive
- Space major events appropriately
- Give promotions room to breathe
- Balance community and revenue
- Prepare staff earlier
- Build marketing ahead
- Identify seasonal opportunities
- Protect owner travel and downtime
- Avoid event fatigue
- Connect promotions to what members are already experiencing

---

## The mix: a good year isn't 12 promotions

A calendar should feel balanced, not transactional. Every entry should belong to a category, and the
categories should be roughly in proportion across the year.

| Category | What it's for | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Community** | Bringing people together, no ask attached | Breakfast events, socials, outdoor workouts, member gatherings, team challenges |
| **Charity** | The community rallying around something bigger than the gym | Fundraisers, cause-based challenges |
| **Competition** | Letting members test themselves — serious or playful | Internal competitions, external events |
| **Promotion** | Intentional campaigns for revenue, leads, referrals or commitment | Referral pushes, seasonal offers |
| **Member Appreciation** | Rewarding, recognising or surprising existing members | Anniversary shoutouts, surprise perks |
| **Marketing / Retail** | Dates the calendar reacts to rather than creates | Valentine's, Mother's Day, Black Friday |
| **Closure / Holiday** | Days you're shut or running reduced hours | Public holidays, maintenance days |
| **Travel** | Owner and staff time away | Conferences, races, vacation |
| **Operations** | Internal work that consumes capacity | Renovations, equipment installs, systems changes |

The first five are the ones you *create*. The last four are the ones you *account for* — they're the
constraints that make the first five realistic.

> The magic is in the mix.

## Don't copy someone else's year

Different community. Different weather. Different members. Different slow months. Different staff.
Different business goals. Different opportunities.

The lesson from anyone else's calendar is never **which** events they chose. It's **how they chose
where those events belong.**

## The Seven Steps

Work these in order. Each one narrows the next.

### Step 1 — The Non-Negotiables

Everything that is already true about the year, before you have an opinion about it.

- Holidays
- Closures
- Major competitions
- Annual events
- Travel
- Local events
- Known facility projects

These go on the calendar first because they're immovable. Everything else has to fit around them.

### Step 2 — Mark Your Seasons

Before placing anything new, characterise each month. Your calendar already has a personality —
name it.

- Strong months
- Slow months
- High attrition periods
- Weather opportunities
- Vacation periods
- Busy community periods

Use these prompts, month by month:

| Lens | Ask |
|---|---|
| **Weather** | Can you be outside? Does weather hurt attendance? Are people travelling? |
| **Member behaviour** | Are members motivated? Busy? On vacation? Coming back into routine? |
| **Sales** | Are leads historically strong or weak? |
| **Retention** | Are cancellations or holds more common? |
| **Community** | What is naturally happening locally? |
| **Operations** | Are staff away? Is this a good time for renovations or upgrades? |

### Step 3 — Add Your Anchors

The major events or campaigns you already know you want to run. Place these **approximately one
year ahead**.

### Step 4 — Balance The Mix

Look at the category spread across the whole year. Not every category needs to appear every month —
but no category should be missing from the year, and no month should be nothing but Promotion.

### Step 5 — Fill Strategic Gaps

Look at the empty periods and ask what the business or the community actually needs there.

| If the month is… | Consider… |
|---|---|
| A slow lead month | A promotion |
| Historically higher attrition | A retention or community event |
| Great weather | Moving something outside |
| Approaching a major competition | Education, programming, retail and content that support it |
| Right after a huge event | Nothing. Deliberately. |
| Following repeated asks to spend | Not asking again yet |

### Step 6 — Connect The Surrounding Marketing

Your calendar should talk to itself. Events, marketing, promotions, retail and programming shouldn't
feel like separate ideas. For each anchor, ask:

- What should we talk about?
- What could we teach?
- Is there a relevant promotion?
- Is there relevant retail?
- What content should we capture?
- What should happen before?
- What should happen afterward?

Worked example — a running event:

```
Upcoming running event → Running education → Running-focused content
    → Relevant retail → Community run → Event day
```

Worked example — a competition season:

```
Competition season → Training focus → Educational content
    → Community hype → Relevant merchandise → Competition → Celebration
```

When the pieces relate to each other, marketing feels natural instead of constant.

> Don't ask "What should we sell this month?"
> Ask "What is already happening in our community, and what naturally belongs beside it?"

### Step 7 — Zoom Out Again

With the year populated, step back and interrogate it:

- Are major events too close together?
- Are we constantly asking members to spend?
- Is there enough community?
- Are slow months supported?
- Are staff going to be exhausted?
- Is there enough lead time?
- Are there periods where we should deliberately do less?

## The Rule

> **Big things: 1 year out. Small things: ~2 months out.**

Plan enough to create clarity. Leave enough room to take advantage of opportunities.

This isn't about predicting every Tuesday next October. It's about making sure October doesn't
surprise you.

## Work backwards: the event date is not the planning date

Say your event is **August 15**. Every supporting piece needs its own date too:

| Date | What happens |
|---|---|
| Earlier in the year | Event already visible on the whole-year calendar |
| May | Event concept finalised |
| June | Creative, operations and staffing |
| July | Registration and promotion |
| August 1 | Final member communication |
| **August 15** | **Event** |

Once the date exists, everything else can have a date too.

**In the tool:** this is automated. Every event carries a set of *milestones* — offsets measured in
weeks before the start date. Each category ships with a sensible default ladder, and you can edit
the ladder per event. Milestones render on the strip as small markers ahead of the block, so lead
time is visible rather than assumed. See [[lead-times]].

## The 90-Minute Meeting

Put 90 minutes on the calendar. Once a year, ideally in the quarter before the year you're planning.

**Bring:**

- Last year's calendar
- Monthly revenue
- Lead numbers
- Attrition
- Attendance
- Local event dates
- Competition dates
- Holiday calendar
- Staff travel
- A laptop
- A printed copy of the calendar

**Run it in three blocks:**

| Block | Focus | Maps to |
|---|---|---|
| First 30 minutes | Understand the year | Steps 1–2 |
| Next 30 minutes | Place the major anchors | Steps 3–4 |
| Final 30 minutes | Look for gaps, conflicts and opportunities | Steps 5–7 |

You don't need to finish every detail. You need to leave knowing what's coming.

---

> Time is going to pass anyway. Know what's coming.
